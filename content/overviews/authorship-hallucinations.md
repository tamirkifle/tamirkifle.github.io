Dreaming Up Authors asks why language models invent author names. The hypothesis is that hallucination tracks factual prevalence: a fact that appears rarely in training data is one a model is more likely to fabricate. Citation count stands in for prevalence, which makes the question measurable. The paper was accepted to the EMNLP 2026 main conference.

My part was the data and the evaluation runs. A five-stage filter reduced 9,999,863 Open Academic Graph records to a corpus of 1,921,209 papers, dropping non-research document types, non-English text, malformed author names, and records whose title and first author disagreed with Semantic Scholar. Stratified sampling then drew 9,108 papers across eight disciplines and thirteen citation bins.

Hallucination rate falls as citation count rises, across every field and model tested: above 98% for papers with two citations or fewer, below 37% at the top of the range. Three closed models were scored on the author lists they returned, and two open-source models were probed at their hidden states, where the last prompt token predicts a paper's citation count before generation begins. Citation count is only a proxy — the training corpora are not public, so prevalence is never measured directly.
