Dreaming Up Authors

Factual prevalence and LLM hallucinations

Paper PDFEMNLP 2026 · Main Conference

Dreaming Up Authors asks why language models invent author names when asked who wrote a given paper. Fabrication tracks training-data prevalence: a fact the model saw rarely is one it gets wrong more often. Citation count stands in as the proxy for that prevalence. The paper was accepted to the EMNLP 2026 main conference.

We reduced 9,999,863 Microsoft Open Academic Graph records to 1,921,209 papers through five filters: document type, author count, language, author-name patterns, and title/first-author agreement with Semantic Scholar. Stratified sampling drew 9,108 papers across eight disciplines and thirteen citation bins. GPT-4o, DeepSeek-R1, and Claude Sonnet 4.5 were scored on overlap between returned and true author lists. Qwen3-32B and Mistral-Small-3.2-24B, both open-source, were probed at their hidden states instead, because the closed models do not expose activations; a ridge regression on the last prompt token predicts citation count before generation begins.

Hallucination rate runs above 98% for papers with zero to two citations and below 37% for the most-cited. The negative correlation holds across all eight fields and all three scored models. A multiple-choice version of the same task reaches 79% chance-corrected accuracy where open-ended recall is near zero, pointing at retrieval failure rather than missing knowledge. Citation count is a proxy for training-data prevalence, not a direct measure; the training corpora are not public, so prevalence is never observed.
